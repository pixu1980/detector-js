Privacy & legality

Because fingerprint-grade attributes can be personal data, we take this seriously — and we think you should too.

🔒 DetectorJS never registers, never transmits, never stores anything.

100% client-side. Zero network calls, zero cookies, zero analytics, zero third-party requests.

The attributes DetectorJS computes stay in the memory of the code that runs it — yours, if you run the demo, or your users’, if you embed it. Only you decide what to do with the output. There is no backend, no account, no telemetry endpoint, not even a tracking pixel — this page makes zero network requests.

However, if you use these attributes about your users you carry responsibilities:

  • GDPR (EU) 2016/679 — device identifiers and environment fingerprints can be personal data (Art. 4(1); Recitals 26, 30): handle them under a lawful basis, purpose limitation, data minimisation, storage limitation, transparency (privacy policy) and appropriate security (Art. 5, 25, 32).
  • ePrivacy Directive 2002/58/EC, Art. 5(3) — storing/accessing info on “user equipment” (the essence of fingerprinting) generally requires informed consent; national implementations apply.
  • Italy — D.Lgs. 196/2003 (Codice Privacy) and D.Lgs. 101/2018; Garante provvedimenti on cookies/tracing; plus the national cookie-consent rules.
  • US / international — CCPA/CPRA (California), other U.S. state privacy laws, FTC Section 5 expectations on truthful disclosures; Canada PIPEDA, Brazil LGPD, etc. when applicable to your audience.
  • Academic background — “How Unique Is Your Web Browser?” (Eckersley, 2010, EFF) showed ~94% of browsers are uniquely identifiable by fingerprints, and “Cookieless Monster” (Acar et al., 2013) demonstrated the privacy risks — the best practice is to avoid persistence & linkage, which is exactly what this library does by design.
